Latest significant finding: Pass 27 — 2026-10-04 · Latest pass: Pass 27 — 2026-10-04

Tucker’s 5th-great-grandfather fought in the Revolution without ever being in a battle — Washington appointed him to draw the camps and battlefields instead.

Pass 27 (2026-10-04): The oldest unanswered question in this vault is answered, and the answer is a surprise. Pvt. William Tarbell had been carried for five passes as a name with a pension citation; four separate passes wrote off his service record as locked behind a paid Fold3 or NARA login. It was free the whole time, in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts’s own published compilation from its state archives — Massachusetts Soldiers and Sailors of the Revolutionary War, Vol. 15 (1907) — filed under the spelling TARBLE, which is why every earlier search missed him.

The record is remarkably complete. He was 16 years old, a Groton farm boy — “stature, 5 ft. 4 in.; complexion, light; hair, light; eyes, light; occupation, farmer; birthplace, Groton; residence, Groton” — when he took a bounty of 300 Spanish milled dollars from his town’s Class No. 12 and enlisted on March 20, 1781 for three years. He served as a private in Capt. Eliphalet Thorp’s company, Lieut. Col. John Brooks’s 7th Massachusetts Regiment, and the muster rolls track him month by month: West Point → Phillipsburgh → Peekskill → “York Hutts” → West Point, and on to Sept. 1783 in the 4th Massachusetts. Those are the Hudson Highlands and the Westchester lines, on garrison duty — he was not at Yorktown. A separate court-martial list adds a very human footnote: on Oct. 5, 1781 he was tried for stealing a fowl and cabbage, or suffering it to be done, while on guard — and acquitted.

Then his 1851 Boston death notice explains why he saw no fighting: he “was with General Washington during the last three years of the war, but having been appointed to draw plans and paint sketches of the various battle fields and encampments, by the commander-in-chief, he was never in any action during that time.” He was a camp draftsman. The notice wrongly places him at Valley Forge — he was 13 that winter — but Groton’s own historian, Samuel A. Green, corrects it and records the real artifact: in the summer of 1783 William Tarbell drew the encampment at New Windsor, just above West Point, where he was then serving, showing the barracks and other camp features. His son had it in Boston in 1851; that son’s widow still had it in 1900. A drawing by Tucker’s 5th-great-grandfather may still exist.

Green’s book also names his parents — Benjamin and Azubah (Farnsworth) Tarbell, Tucker’s 6th-great-grandparents — from Groton’s own records, calls William their second son, and records that Azubah died in 1838 at the age of 97 years, 8 months and 19 days, the oldest person in Groton at the time. His first wife is corrected to Polly Simonds of Groton, married April 8, 1788; he married twice more and died at Groton on Aug. 3, 1851, aged 86. And the 1841 federal Census of Pensioners lists him plainly — “William Tarbell. 76.” — so the family’s pensioner claim now rests on a free federal document. Best of all, Caleb Butler’s History of Groton (1848) — written while William was alive, calling him “now of Groton, a revolutionary pensioner” — traces him back four generations in print, through Benjamin, through the William who married Mary Farnsworth, to the Thomas who was town clerk in 1704–05 and “father of John and Zachariah, the lads who were captured by the Indians.” That is the 1707 captivity story from Pass 23, and it means the colonial chain is now confirmed by a source independent of every one used before.

Pass 26 (2026-09-27): Mabel’s parent pair is finally complete, and it came from a city directory rather than a gravestone. The 1913 R. L. Polk Columbus city directory lists, in the same household at 459 N. Garfield Avenue, both “Richards Mabel, phys 314½ E Broad, b 459 N Garfield av” and “Richards Rebecca N (wid Harvey), h 459 N Garfield av.” Polk’s abbreviations are exact — h means keeps house, b means boards — so Mabel was living in the house kept by Rebecca N. Richards, the widow of Harvey. Last week’s pass proved from Mabel’s own gravestone that her father was Harvey Richards, so her mother is Rebecca N. Richards, and Harvey was already dead by 1913. The same directory shows Mabel in the classified Physicians list with her own office at 314½ East Broad Street — she was practicing on her own account in Columbus the year before she married. And it carries “Tarbell Robt C, phys 246 E State, b 259 S 18th,” putting Robert C. Tarbell in the same city that year, boarding at the exact address recorded in 1945 for his sister Mrs. Julia Tarbell Buchanan — a contemporaneous corroboration of that sibling link, and a nice picture of the couple: two young physicians with separate offices a mile apart on Broad and State, a year before the wedding. A fourth family member surfaced too — Margaret M. Richards, widow of Lester — matching Lester D. Richards’ death on Dec. 26, 1911. One correction: the marriage index flagged last week belongs to Franklin County, Texas, not Ohio, so the 1914 Tarbell–Richards license has to come from the Ohio History Connection’s Franklin County microform instead.

Pass 25 (2026-09-20): A direct hit on the Mabel question. Dr. Mabel R. Tarbell’s Green Lawn Cemetery interment record (Columbus, Franklin County, Ohio) is now captured verbatim, and it resolves two long-open questions at once. The transcription reads “Tarbell, Mabel R (Dr) — b. 04 Mar 1886 — d. 28 Sep 1967 — father: Richards, Harvey.” That names her father as Harvey (A.) Richards — upgrading last week’s unproven snippet hint to a sourced transcription fact — and pins her exact vitals (dgcotton had only “~1887” for her birth; the death date matches dgcotton). Her middle initial R is her maiden name, Richards, carried through. A second Green Lawn row corroborates the father: Lester D. Richards (1878–1911), father “Richards, Harvey A.” — a probable brother of Mabel’s. This also overturns the Pass 14 negative (“no Tarbell in any Franklin County cemetery”): Mabel was indexed there all along, under her married name. Robert C. Tarbell still lies at Confidence Cemetery in Georgetown, so husband and wife rest in different counties — which is why a joint-plot search never found them together. Still open: Mabel’s mother (Harvey’s wife), Harvey’s own dates, and the ~Mar. 1914 Robert Tarbell + Mabel Richards marriage record (now with a specific free lead, the Franklin County Genealogical Society by-year marriage index).

Pass 24 (2026-09-13): The newest pass strengthens the Mabel Richards proof rather than replacing the Pass 23 headline. OSU Libraries / Medical Heritage Center holds the physical Starling-Ohio Medical College M.D. diploma for Mabel Sara Richards, Dr., dated May 20, 1908. OSU’s Medical Heritage Center blog also says Starling-Ohio’s first two women graduates were Mabel S. Richards and Bessie Lucretia Sweet in 1908. That independently supports Pass 22’s dgcotton finding that Dr. Robert Campbell Tarbell’s wife was Mabel Richards, and ties cleanly to Pass 16’s Worthington News record of Dr. Mabel S. Tarbell as a practicing psychiatrist. Best current identity: Dr. Mabel Sara Richards Tarbell. Still open: her parents and the Robert Campbell Tarbell + Mabel Richards marriage record.

Pass 23 (2026-09-06): Two more generations back, and one remarkable story forward. The Groton Tarbell line now runs all the way to the English immigrant Thomas Tarbell — born 1615 in Romsey, Hampshire, England, and died in 1678 at Charlestown, Massachusetts of smallpox — the ancestor of the whole New-England Tarbell family, who settled Watertown by 1647 and moved to Groton in 1663. He reaches Tucker through his son Thomas Tarbell Jr. (also died in the 1678 smallpox epidemic) and grandson Deacon Thomas Tarbell III. That makes the immigrant couple Tucker’s 10th-great-grandparents — the deepest the tree has ever reached, and his first confirmed English birthplace on this branch. This pass also adds Samuel Woods + Alice Rushton, parents of Deacon Thomas’s wife Elizabeth Woods (9th-great-grandparents), and fills in Deacon Thomas himself as Groton’s town clerk (1704–05) and church deacon. The headline, though, is a story: on June 20, 1707, during Queen Anne’s War, a Mohawk war party from Kahnawake abducted three of Deacon Thomas + Elizabeth (Woods) Tarbell’s children while they picked cherries. Sarah (~14) was ransomed to a Montreal convent; the two boys, John (12) and Zachariah (7), were adopted into Mohawk families, married chiefs’ daughters, became chiefs themselves, and in the 1750s helped found Akwesasne / St. Regis — remembered ever since as the “Tarbell chiefs.” Tucker’s direct ancestor, Lt. William Tarbell (born 1689), was their older brother, who stayed in Groton and carried the line forward. And the Revolutionary ancestor from Pass 22, Pvt. William Tarbell (1764–1851), is confirmed as a documented war pensioner and the family’s DAR patriot; born in 1764 he was too young for the 1775 muster, so the older Tarbells on the Lexington Alarm rolls were his relatives. (His younger brother, Capt. Joseph Tarbell, became a noted U.S. Navy officer.) Two cautions: the immigrant’s 1615 Romsey birthplace still rests on compiled family trees and wants a primary record, and this 1707 raid is a different event from the well-known 1694 raid on Groton.

Pass 27 — Pvt. William Tarbell’s actual war record, his parents, and a drawing he made of Washington’s last camp (2026-10-04) Latest

The vault’s oldest carry-over was Pvt. William Tarbell’s Revolutionary War service — open since Pass 23 and written off in four consecutive passes as needing a paid Fold3 or NARA login. This pass tried a different lane: the Commonwealth of Massachusetts’s own published compilation from its state archives, Massachusetts Soldiers and Sailors of the Revolutionary War (17 volumes, free full text). Surnames Ta–Tz are volume 15; its OCR was downloaded and parsed locally, yielding 34 Tarbell-variant entries, seven of them a William. Tucker’s ancestor sits under the spelling TARBLE, with a second entry under TARBELL — one man split across two headwords, which is exactly why every spelling-exact search in five passes came up empty.

The descriptive list is the prize: age, 17 yrs.; stature, 5 ft. 4 in.; complexion, light; hair, light; eyes, light; occupation, farmer; birthplace, Groton; residence, Groton — enough to confirm identity against his known Oct. 1764 birth without a vital record. He enlisted for the town of Groton on March 20, 1781 (mustered the 28th) for three years, a private in Capt. Eliphalet Thorp’s company, Lieut. Col. John Brooks’s 7th Massachusetts Regiment, later the regiment’s 4th company and then Capt. Nathaniel C. Allen’s 8th company, 4th Massachusetts, through September 1783. The muster rolls are dated West Point, Phillipsburgh, Peekskill, “York Hutts,” West Point — the Hudson Highlands and Westchester lines, on garrison duty, so he was not at Yorktown. That result pointed back to Samuel A. Green’s Groton During the Revolution (1900) — already cited in this vault for the 1707 captives, but never read for William. It carries a full biographical notice: his parents, Benjamin and Azubah (Farnsworth) Tarbell (Tucker’s 6th-great-grandparents, previously resting on one compiler’s tree), William their second son; Azubah dead on March 14, 1838 at 97 years, 8 months and 19 days — the oldest person in Groton, which computes to a birth about June 24, 1740 and matches the compiled tree’s 1740 exactly; his first wife corrected from “Mary ‘Molly Simonds’ Simmons” to Polly Simonds of Groton, married April 8, 1788; two later marriages (Susan Blood 1823, Mrs. Sarah Wetherbee Nutting 1840); and his death at Groton on Aug. 3, 1851, aged 86 years 9 months 16 days. The headline is his job. His 1851 Boston death notice says he “was with General Washington during the last three years of the war, but having been appointed to draw plans and paint sketches of the various battle fields and encampments, by the commander-in-chief, he was never in any action during that time.” Green corrects the notice’s Valley Forge claim — William was 13 that winter — but records the real artifact: in the summer of 1783 he drew the encampment at New Windsor, just above West Point, where he was then serving, showing the barracks and other camp features.
